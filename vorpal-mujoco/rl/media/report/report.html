Arachne hexapod · simulation results · 18 Nov 2026

Following a path with dead servos, and saying which servo failed

MuJoCo (MJX) simulation of the 12-servo Arachne hexapod. One reinforcement-learning policy, trained once, follows a given path to a goal while servos fail mid-walk. A separate network reads the same sensor history (IMU only, no joint sensing) and names the failed servo.

Summary

  1. With three servos dead mid-walk, the policy trained with faults reaches the goal in 87.7% of 512 test runs, against 80.7% for the same policy trained without faults, and gets there 1.8 s sooner (median). It never fell over.
  2. With one faulty servo of any kind, both policies reach the goal every time. A six-legged robot with one bad servo still has a stable gait, so single faults are not where the challenge is on flat ground.
  3. From 3 s of IMU and command history, the diagnosis network detects 85% of faults (median 2.6 s after onset, 0.22 false alarms per minute) and names the faulty servo 63% of the time (chance: 8%). It is good on dead and stuck servos and weak on deadband and on telling fault kinds apart.
  4. The better the policy compensates, the harder the fault is to see: the same diagnosis method named the right servo 77% of the time on the earlier policy and 63% on the fault-trained one. This trade-off is an argument for training diagnosis and control together, the planned next step.

Setup

Robot and simulator. Arachne: six legs, a hip and a knee servo per leg (12 MG90S position servos), an MPU-6050 IMU, no joint-angle sensing. Simulated in MuJoCo MJX on a laptop GPU (RTX 4060), 8,192 parallel environments, 20 Hz control.

Task. Each episode draws a random path (20 cm pieces, straight or curved down to a 33 cm radius) and a goal 1.0–1.9 m along it. The policy sees the next path points in its own body frame, its distance off the path and the distance left. Episodes last 20 s.

Faults. 0–3 servos per episode fail between 0.5 and 8 s, chosen at random. Five kinds:

Policies compared. trained with faults (63M steps) and trained healthy only (31M steps) on the same task, network and reward. Both use only what the real robot has: one second of IMU readings, the last commands sent to the servos, and the path features. The critic sees the true fault labels during training only.

Test. 512 fixed test cases per scenario, the same paths and start poses for every policy. The goal is 1.5 m along the path. The first fault starts at 1 s; extra faults follow 2 s apart. "Dead" means frozen or limp, on different legs.

3. Saying which servo failed

A separate network (an MLP on 3 s of the policy's own input) outputs, for each of the 12 servos, healthy or one of the five fault kinds. It was trained on 2,457 episodes of the fault-trained policy with random faults and tested on 615 held-out episodes. Servo and kind scores are for windows with exactly one faulty servo, at least 1 s after the fault started, while the robot is still walking.

A longer history mattered most: with the policy's own 1 s window, the earlier policy's servo accuracy was 43%; with 3 s it was 77%. The drop from 77% to 63% on the fault-trained policy is consistent with that policy hiding faults better, though its test data also has more two- and three-fault episodes, which may account for part of the drop.

What changed today

  • First test was not valid. The first policies (v1) had goals 0.4–0.8 m away and walked about 0.2 m/s, so they arrived in about 3 s, before most faults started. The task now uses 1.0–1.9 m goals, faults that start mid-walk, and up to three faulty servos (v2).
  • Diagnosis history 1 s → 3 s. Servo accuracy on the v1 policy went from 43% to 77% while walking.
  • Lost runs. The laptop ran out of RAM twice (14 GB, with a browser open), killing two training runs. Jobs now run one at a time as separate system services with memory caps.

Limits of these results

  • Simulation only, flat ground, idealised position servos. Nothing here has been run on the real robot.
  • The diagnosis network is trained after the policy and does not feed back into it. The policy adapts on its own, from the same sensor history; it does not act on an explicit "servo X is dead" signal yet.
  • The 20 s limit makes "goal reached" partly a speed measure. On flat ground, faults slow the robot more than they topple it.
  • One training seed per policy. The 7-point gap at three dead servos is about three standard errors over 512 paired runs, but it has not been checked across seeds.
  • The simulated walking speed (about 0.2 m/s) is probably faster than the real MG90S robot can manage.

Next steps

  1. Train diagnosis together with the policy (teacher–student, with a diagnosis head on the student), so the policy can use what the diagnosis knows.
  2. Rough terrain and slopes, where a dead leg should matter far more than on flat ground.
  3. Repeat the comparison over 3 seeds and add a "told which servo is dead" upper bound.
  4. Demo: a top-down camera, a path drawn by hand on the laptop, and a panel that disables servos live.
